# AI tells in English customer copy

Adapted for local-service web copy from blader/humanizer v3.1.0 (MIT,
https://github.com/blader/humanizer, checked 2026-10-06), which itself draws on
Wikipedia's "Signs of AI writing" advice page
(https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing).

Why the tells exist: a model writes the most likely next word, so by default it makes the
choice that fits the widest range of readers. A person writes for one reader and one job,
so their choices are uneven and specific. Every pattern below is a default choice. The
fix is almost always a specific fact from the input, not a cleverer phrase. If the input
has no fact for that sentence, the sentence usually goes.

The order is strongest first. Sections 1-5 justify an edit on one sighting; the rest need
company from other tells before you act.

All "after" examples are illustrations. Their details stand for facts that would come
from the owner. Never copy a detail from this file into client copy.

## 1. Staged contrast ("not X but Y")

Watch for: "It's not just a removal, it's a fresh start"; "not only... but also";
"X rather than Y" where nobody claimed X; the same contrast split over two sentences;
a clipped tail (", no hidden fees").
Why: the negative half names something nobody said, so the positive half sounds bigger
without adding a claim. Keep a contrast only when it corrects a belief the customer
really holds ("Most people think you need to empty the drawers. You don't.").
Before: We don't just move furniture, we move lives.
After: We wrap and move the furniture; you only need to pack the small things.

## 2. One-line closers and fragment rows

Watch for: a short paragraph that repeats the one before ("That's the difference.");
"Simple as that."; a row of fragments ("No fuss. No stress. No surprises.").
Why: it asks the reader to pause on a point instead of adding to it. A short sentence is
fine when it carries a new fact.

## 3. Staged run-ups and arguing with no one

Watch for: "Here's the thing", "Let's be honest", "Wondering whether...? Look no
further", "You might think... but", "To be clear".
Why: the copy announces a point instead of making it. Start with the point.

## 4. Inflated significance and sales language

Watch for: "plays a key role", "a testament to", "nestled in the heart of", "vibrant
community", "rich history", "second to none", "unparalleled", "seamless",
"stress-free", "peace of mind", "every step of the way".
Why: it dresses an ordinary fact as important. Local pages are the worst case: "the
vibrant city of Bristol" tells a Bristol reader nothing.
Before: Nestled in the heart of Clifton, our dedicated team delivers a seamless,
stress-free experience every step of the way.
After: We work across Clifton and Redland most weeks. On the steep streets we bring the
smaller van, so it can park closer to the door.

## 5. Borrowed authority and vague proof

Watch for: "trusted by thousands", "highly rated", "industry-leading", "experts agree",
"award-winning" without the award, "fully insured" without the cover.
Why: proof without a source is exactly what CAP 3.7 asks the marketer to back with
documents. Name the source (the review platform, the insurer, the scheme) or cut it.

## 6. Forced threes

Watch for: "fast, friendly and affordable"; three parallel benefit sentences; three
cards with the same shape where the third was invented to fill the row.
Why: ideas arrive in threes to sound complete, whether the meaning has three parts or
not. Keep three when there are three real things; otherwise use the true number.

## 7. Dashes as the universal connector

Watch for: em and en dashes, spaced hyphens and double hyphens used as dashes.
Why: a dash lets the writer skip deciding how two clauses relate. In customer copy on
Soborbo sites they are not used at all (house rule); choose a full stop, comma, colon or
brackets, and a plain hyphen in ranges.

## 8. Formatting by rule

Watch for: Title Case Headings On Every Section; a bold label and colon starting every
bullet ("**Reliable:** we turn up on time"); an icon or emoji on every item; a heading
that the first sentence repeats word for word.
Why: formatting applied to everything stops signalling anything. Use sentence case,
let headings name what the section holds, and use bold rarely.

## 9. Avoiding "is", "are", "has"

Watch for: "serves as", "stands as", "represents", "boasts", "features", "offers a
wide range of".
Before: Our Bristol depot serves as the hub for our operations and boasts a fleet of
vehicles.
After: We work from a depot in [area from siteConfig] and have [number from owner] vans.

## 10. Shallow "-ing" riders and stacked hedges

Watch for: a sentence that ends in ", ensuring...", ", providing...", ", making it...";
"could potentially", "may help to", "in some cases it may".
Why: the rider claims a benefit without saying what happens. Either state the concrete
effect or drop it.

## 11. Writing about the page instead of the service

Watch for: "In this guide we'll explore", "Below you'll find", "As mentioned above",
"This page explains".
Why: the reader can see the page. Start with the content.

## 12. Leftovers

Watch for: "I hope this helps", "Certainly!", "Here is a revised version", "as of my
last update", placeholder brackets that look like content.
Why: these come from the chat, not the page. They must never reach a live site.

## Words that often signal generated copy

Use as a second pass only, after the structural tells: delve, tapestry, landscape
(figurative), leverage, foster, harness, pivotal, meticulous, robust, comprehensive,
cutting-edge, elevate, embark, realm, testament, navigate (figurative), unlock,
game-changer, furthermore, moreover, additionally. Any of these can be the right word in
context ("comprehensive cover" is a real insurance term). One alone proves nothing.

## What stays

Leave these alone even if they look like a tell:

- a real, specific detail (a street, an odd job, a named supplier) that came from the owner;
- a genuine aside or self-correction in the owner's own text;
- quotations, review text, product names and legal wording;
- anything in the owner's own writing sample that the sample uses on purpose.
